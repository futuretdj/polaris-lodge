Days& nightshere

The aurora is the reason people come. It is not the only thing they remember.

In the few hours of blue daylight there are huskies to run, reindeer to feed, a frozen lake to fish through and a Sámi village with a story older than any border here. In the evening there is a fire, a long dinner, and then the sky.

This page explains everything properly: what each trip is like hour by hour, what you will eat, how the aurora actually works, and what every month in Inari feels like.

A January day,
hour by hour

In mid-January the sun barely rises — it stays just below the horizon and paints the sky pink and blue for five hours. This is a real day from our log, 16 January.

08:30

Blue darkness and coffee

You wake up without an alarm. Outside it is still dark blue, −22 °C, completely still. Breakfast in the main house: rye bread from the village bakery, cloudberry jam, smoked whitefish, porridge with lingonberries, and coffee strong enough to wake a reindeer. The night host tells everyone what the sky did last night.

Sky: deep blue · Kp 3 forecast tonight
10:30

The pink hours begin

The horizon turns pink, then orange, then lilac — and stays that way until two. This is the best light of the day for photographs. Most guests go out now: the husky safari leaves from the yard, the snowshoe trail starts at the door, and the ice-fishing sled waits on the lake.

Sky: polar twilight · sun 1.5° below horizon
13:00

Soup by the fire

Lunch is simple and hot: salmon soup with dill, or reindeer stew, eaten in a kota by the lake with a fire in the middle. Outside, the light slowly turns from pink to blue. Your cheeks will be red; that is the Lapland colour.

Sky: blue hour starts at 13:40
15:00

Smoke sauna and the ice hole

The smoke sauna has been heating since eight in the morning. Three rounds: heat, then thirty steps across the snow to a hole in the lake ice, one degree above zero, then back into the heat. It sounds impossible. Everyone does it, and everyone does it again.

Sky: dark · first stars at 15:30
18:30

A long dinner

Five courses from the lake, the forest and the reindeer herders we know by name. Candles, no music, big windows facing north. Halfway through the main course somebody always stands up and goes to the window — usually too early.

Sky: clear · magnetometer rising
21:00

The night host starts the watch

The lights of the main house go off. The host goes up to the terrace with a thermos and a radio. You read, sleep, or lie under your glass roof and wait. The forest makes no sound at all at −25 °C.

Sky: Kp 4 · solar wind 520 km/s
23:51

Two short rings

The room phone. You pull on the overalls, walk down to the shore. A pale arc lies over the lake, then it brightens, starts to move, breaks into rays and rolls across the sky from east to west. Eleven minutes. Then it fades — and comes back twice more before two.

Sky: aurora · Kp 6 · logged as entry № 0412

Pirkko’s team, 10:40, −18 °C

Twenty kilometres through the forest and over the frozen lake behind your own team of six dogs. The moment they are released, the howling stops and there is only the sound of the runners on the snow.

We work with Pirkko and Jussi, a family kennel twenty minutes away with 62 Alaskan huskies. You drive the sled yourself — two people per sled, one standing and steering, one sitting wrapped in a reindeer skin — and you swap halfway. Steering is easier than it looks: a ten-minute lesson is enough. The dogs know the trail better than anyone.

Ante’s herd, near Kaamanen

A quiet evening with Ante, a Sámi reindeer herder whose family has kept reindeer around Inari for at least seven generations. No show, no costumes — just his farm, his animals and his stories.

You feed the reindeer lichen from your hands (they are shy, then suddenly very brave), then take a slow sleigh ride by lantern light through the forest — a single reindeer pulls each sleigh, walking pace, perfectly silent. Afterwards, coffee boiled over the fire in Ante’s kota, dried reindeer meat, and an hour of stories: how herding works today, the eight seasons of the Sámi year, and why every reindeer in Lapland belongs to someone.

A quiet bay of Lake Inari, blue hour

Lake Inari is the third-largest lake in Finland, has more than 3,000 islands, and from January to April you can walk on it. Under the ice live whitefish, perch and the arctic char that ends up on our dinner menu.

We cross the ice by snowmobile sled to a quiet bay, drill holes with a hand auger (try it — it is good exercise) and sit on reindeer skins with short rods and a lot of patience. Our guide Esa grew up fishing here and knows where the perch gather. Whatever we catch, we cook over the fire on a wooden plank right there on the ice.

Tykky — snow crowns on the pines

A slow walk through a forest where every tree is wrapped in snow — the Finns call it tykky — to the top of Otsamo fell, 400 metres above the lake, with a view over half of Lapland.

Snowshoes let you walk on top of a metre of fresh snow without sinking. The path climbs gently; we stop often to look at tracks (fox, hare, ptarmigan, sometimes a wolverine), to take photos in the pink light, and to drink something hot. At the top there is an open shelter with a fire place, and on a clear day you can see Norway.

A fire in the old smoke cabin

Inari is the heart of Sámi culture in Finland — home to the Sámi Parliament and to three Sámi languages. This day is about understanding the people who have lived here for thousands of years, from the people themselves.

We start at Siida, the Sámi museum, where a Sámi guide walks you through the exhibition about the eight seasons and the life of the land. Dinner at
Kammi

Kammi means “hut” in the old local dialect. It is our dining room: eight tables, big windows facing north, candles, and food from within a hundred kilometres. Chef Laura Kitti changes the menu every week, depending on what the lake, the forest and the herders give us.

FirstLohikeittoCreamy salmon soup, dill, warm rye breadstory +

Every Finnish grandmother has her own version; this one is Laura’s grandmother’s, from Ivalo. Salmon from the Teno river, potatoes and leeks from a farm in Kittilä, a little cream, a lot of dill, and black pepper added at the very end.

The bread is baked every morning by the village bakery — dark, sour, dense rye, served warm with salted butter.

Pairing: Lapland birch-sap sparkling (non-alcoholic)

SecondArctic char on the plankFire-cooked, smoked butter, pickled onionstory +

Char is the king of Lake Inari: cold-water fish with pink, delicate flesh. Esa brings it from the lake in the morning. We nail the fillet to a board of alder wood and cook it slowly beside an open fire — the old Sámi way called loimulohi.

It comes to the table on the plank, still smelling of smoke, with butter we smoke ourselves and sweet pickled red onion to cut through the richness.

Pairing: Riesling Kabinett, Mosel

ThirdPoronkäristysSautéed reindeer, butter mash, lingonberries, pickled cucumberstory +

The dish Lapland is famous for. Thinly shaved reindeer meat from Ante’s herd, cooked slowly in butter with onion until it is soft and deeply savoury. It is served the only correct way: on a mountain of buttery potato mash, with sour lingonberries and a slice of pickled cucumber.

Reindeer here live free in the forest and eat lichen, so the meat is lean and tastes faintly of the forest. We use every part of the animal, and the skins become the throws in the Kota Loft.

Pairing: Pinot Noir, Baden — or a Finnish craft porter

FourthLeipäjuusto & cloudberriesWarm “squeaky” cheese, cloudberry sauce, cardamom creamstory +

Leipäjuusto — “bread cheese” — is a mild fresh cheese baked until golden. It squeaks against your teeth, which is why everyone calls it squeaky cheese. Traditionally it was dried and kept for months, then warmed in coffee.

We serve it warm with cloudberries — the orange “gold of the north”, which grow only in the bogs of Lapland in August and cannot be farmed. Every jar is picked by hand by local families.

Pairing: Lakka cloudberry liqueur, served cold

LastFire, coffee and something sweetKuksa coffee by the fireplace, rye and spruce-tip cookiesstory +

Dinner ends in the lounge, by the fire. Coffee is served in a kuksa — a cup carved from a single birch burl, which a Sámi tradition says should be given, never bought. Ours are carved by a craftsman in Utsjoki.

This is usually the moment the night host walks in with the forecast for the night. If the numbers are good, the room goes very quiet.

An honest guide
to the aurora

Light from the Sun, drawn by the Earth

The Sun constantly blows a wind of charged particles into space. When this solar wind reaches the Earth, our magnetic field guides some of it down towards the poles, into a ring-shaped zone called the auroral oval. Inari lies right under that oval.

About 100 to 300 km above the ground, the particles hit oxygen and nitrogen in the thin upper air and make it glow — the same physics as a neon sign. Oxygen low down glows green, oxygen very high glows red, and nitrogen gives the purple and pink edges you see when the aurora moves fast.

Because the light comes from so high up, the aurora can be seen from hundreds of kilometres away, and it always appears to the north first. Strong displays then spread overhead and even to the south.

It is happening during the day too — you just cannot see it against the sunlight. That is why the season here runs from late August to early April, when the nights are dark. In May, June and July the sun barely sets and the aurora is invisible.

To your eye, a weak aurora often looks like a pale grey-green cloud. Cameras see colour better than eyes do in the dark, so photos often look more colourful than the sky — but a strong display is unmistakably green, bright and moving, and nothing in a photo prepares you for it.

How to read the forecast

The Kp index measures how disturbed the Earth’s magnetic field is, from 0 to 9. The higher it is, the brighter the aurora and the further south it reaches.

Here at 69° north, the aurora is right above us, so even a small number is often enough. What matters more is clear sky — the clouds decide more than the Sun.

We watch three things every night: the cloud radar, the Kp forecast, and the live magnetometer at the Kevo research station, 90 km north. When its line suddenly drops, the aurora usually starts within twenty minutes.

0–1Weak arc on the northern horizon. Common.
2–3Clear green arc, sometimes rays. Visible here on most clear nights.
4–5Bright, moving curtains overhead. A great night.
6–7Whole sky in motion, purple edges, coronas. A few times a season.
8–9Red aurora, seen as far south as Germany. Rare, unforgettable.

With a phone or a camera

You do not need expensive equipment. You need something steady, a long exposure, and patience with cold fingers.

The biggest mistake is to wait for the perfect moment: start shooting as soon as you see anything. The second biggest is to forget to look up and simply enjoy it — put the camera down for at least a few minutes.

  1. Make it steadyUse a tripod, or lean the phone against a rock, a fence or your bag. Any movement blurs the stars.
  2. Turn on night modeOn a phone, choose night mode and the longest exposure — 3 to 10 seconds. Turn the flash off.
  3. On a camera: f/2.8, 4 s, ISO 1600–3200Shorter exposure when the aurora moves fast, longer when it is slow and faint.
  4. Focus at infinityFocus on a bright star or a distant light, then switch to manual focus and do not touch the ring again.
  5. Add the groundA tree, a cabin, a person or the frozen lake makes the photo. The sky alone is beautiful but anonymous.
  6. Keep batteries warmCold empties them in minutes. Keep a spare in an inside pocket and swap.

Things people get wrong

We hear them every week. Here are the most common — and what is actually true.

You can only see it in the middle of winterSeptember, October and March are excellent: nights are dark, it is warmer, and lakes are not yet frozen, so the aurora reflects in the water.
It always looks like the photosWeak displays look grey-green to the eye. Strong ones look even better than photos, because they move.
You need a very cold nightCold does not cause the aurora. Clear sky does — and clear nights in winter just happen to be cold.
It only appears at midnightIt can start any time after dark — even at 17:00 in December. The most active hours here are usually between 21:00 and 01:00.
You should whistle at itAn old belief says whistling makes the aurora come down and take you. We do not test it. Neither should you.
